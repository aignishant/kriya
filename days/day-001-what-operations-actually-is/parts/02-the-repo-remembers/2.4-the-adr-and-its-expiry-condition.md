# 2.4 — The ADR, and the expiry condition that makes it honest

## One-line answer

An architecture decision record is a short document saying what you chose, what you rejected and
what would make you change your mind — and it is the last section that separates a decision from a
justification.

---

## The story

🎬 **The scene.** A family is choosing where to live. They pick a place a long way from work because
it is cheap and quiet, and they are pleased with the reasoning. Someone asks why not somewhere
closer. They explain: the commute is tolerable and the saving is large.

😬 **The naive fix.** Write down the reasons. Cheap, quiet, tolerable commute. Everyone agrees, and
the note goes in a drawer.

💥 **Why it fails.** Four years later a child starts school, the commute doubles because a road
closes, and the saving is eaten by fuel. The decision is now clearly wrong. But nobody revisits it,
because the note in the drawer still reads as a perfectly good argument — it is *still true* that the
house was cheap and quiet. Nothing in the note says **under what conditions this choice stops being
right**, so there is no trigger, and a decision without a trigger gets defended instead of reviewed.

💡 **The insight.** The valuable sentence is not "we chose this because…". It is "**we would choose
differently if…**", with something observable in it. That single sentence converts a piece of
self-justification into a decision with an expiry condition — and expiry conditions are what let a
system be changed by people who were not in the room.

---

## The idea in plain language

An **ADR — architecture decision record** — is a short, numbered, immutable document recording one
decision. Immutable in the same sense as the ledgers: you do not rewrite ADR-0003 when you change
your mind, you write ADR-0011 and mark ADR-0003 *superseded*. The chain of ADRs is the reasoning
history of the system.

You do not write one for every choice. The filter this repository uses is stated in
`docs/DECISIONS.md`: **write an ADR when a choice would be expensive to reverse and non-obvious to a
stranger.** Both halves matter. Expensive-but-obvious needs no explanation; non-obvious-but-cheap can
just be changed. It is the intersection that produces the *"why on earth is it like this?"* question
six months later.

This repository requires six sections, and the last two are the ones that make it worth writing:

| Section | The question it answers | What a bad version looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Context** | what was true when we decided? | no numbers, just "we needed something scalable" |
| **Options considered** | what else was on the table? | one option, listed to make the choice look inevitable |
| **Decision** | what did we choose? | a paragraph instead of a sentence |
| **Consequences** | what is now easier, harder, and committed to? | only the good half |
| **What would make us change our minds** | **which observable condition invalidates this?** | "if it stops working for us" |
| **Cold read** | does it still make sense later? | never filled in |

### Why "options considered" is not padding

Because the options you rejected are the part that cannot be recovered. Six months later the code
shows what you built. Nothing shows what you deliberately did *not* build, so a stranger's first
instinct is to propose it — and you will find yourself re-litigating a decision whose reasoning you
have partly forgotten, against someone who is arguing in good faith. Writing down the rejected option
*with its honest advantages* is what ends that conversation in one link.

Note "with its honest advantages". An options table where every rejected option is obviously terrible
is a tell: it means the writer worked backwards from the answer, and it means the reader learns
nothing about the trade.

### Why the expiry condition must contain something observable

Compare:

- *"We would reconsider if this approach stops meeting our needs."* — unfalsifiable. It will never
  trigger, because nobody can ever say the condition has been met.
- *"We would reconsider if the median day exceeds roughly twenty-five parts."* — a number. Someone can
  check it, in the tracker, without asking anyone.

The second is from ADR-0002 in this repository. It is checkable by a person who was not in the room,
which is the entire test.

## The mechanism

Look at a real one, then look at the shape of its most important section.

```bash
ls docs/adr/
grep -A6 "What would make us change our minds" docs/adr/ADR-0002-depth-contract.md
```

**Line by line:**

- `ls docs/adr/` lists the decision records. The filename carries the number **and** a slug, for the
  same reason day folders do: `ADR-0002.md` is an address, `ADR-0002-depth-contract.md` is an answer.
- `grep -A6` prints the matching line plus the six lines after it. `-A` (after) is the flag you reach
  for when the interesting content follows a known heading — the alternative, opening the file and
  scrolling, does not compose into a pipeline and does not work over four hundred files.

Observed on **2026-08-24**:

```text
ADR-0001-project-charter.md
ADR-0002-depth-contract.md
ADR-0003-zero-cost-and-local-first.md
ADR-0004-fundamentals-before-ai.md
```

```text
## What would make us change our minds

- If `./o depth` starts passing days that read badly, the mechanical half is measuring the wrong
  thing and needs a *different* check — never a relaxed one.
- If the median day exceeds roughly twenty-five parts, sections are being used as a substitute for
  day boundaries and the day map itself needs amending, with its own ADR.
```

**Read those two bullets as instructions to a future person.** The first names a *symptom* (days pass
the script and read badly) and pre-commits to a response that is not the tempting one (fix the check,
do not relax it). The second names a *number* someone can look up in `docs/TRACKER.md` without
context. Neither requires the author to still be here.

The lifecycle of an ADR is a small state machine, and it is worth knowing because "superseded" is the
state people forget exists:

```mermaid
stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> proposed: written, not yet agreed
    proposed --> accepted: agreed; the system is built this way
    proposed --> rejected: we decided not to
    accepted --> superseded: a later ADR replaces it
    accepted --> deprecated: no longer applies, nothing replaced it
    superseded --> [*]
    deprecated --> [*]
    rejected --> [*]
    note right of superseded
        the old ADR is never edited or deleted
        the new one names it, the old one links forward
    end note
```

## When it breaks

Two failure modes, and the first is the common one.

**The ADR that is a press release.** Every rejected option is a straw man, the consequences section
lists only benefits, and the expiry condition is *"if requirements change"*. Here is the shape:

```text
## Options considered

| Option | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Build it ourselves | full control | slow, expensive, risky |
| B. The thing we chose | fast, flexible, industry standard | none significant |

## What would make us change our minds

If the solution no longer meets our requirements.
```

**What it says:** we considered alternatives.
**What it actually means:** we did not. "None significant" in a cons column is never true of anything,
and a reader who has ever made a real technical decision will read that table and discount the entire
document. **The smallest fix:** write one genuine disadvantage of the chosen option. If you cannot
think of one, you have not understood it yet, and that is the finding.

**The ADR nobody can find.** The document is excellent and lives in a directory nobody opens, so the
question gets asked *at the code* and the answer sits three directories away. This is
[2.1](2.1-why-the-chat-is-not-the-memory.md)'s proximity problem with better formatting. **The
smallest fix** is a one-line comment at the site of the decision naming the ADR — the code says
*what*, the comment says *see ADR-0007*, the ADR says *why*. Three levels of detail, each findable
from the one above.

This repository already does that. `pyproject.toml` carries a comment explaining `link-mode = "copy"`
with the upstream issue numbers, precisely so that a future reader tidying up the file finds the
reason before deleting the line.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** They write the ADR **before** the
work, not after, and they circulate it while the decision is still reversible. An ADR written
afterwards is a summary; an ADR written beforehand is a design review that costs one document. The
tell that a team is doing this well is that some of their ADRs have status `rejected` — a decision
log with no rejected entries is a log of things that were going to happen anyway.

**What degrades at scale.** Discoverability, and then trust. Forty ADRs is a directory you can skim;
four hundred is a corpus nobody reads, at which point people stop writing them because "nobody reads
them", which is true and self-inflicted. The fixes are the boring ones: an index file with one line
per decision (this repository's `docs/DECISIONS.md`), and links *from the code to the ADR* rather
than only the reverse.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** The decision that was right and quietly stopped
being right. Nothing breaks, no alert fires, and the system slowly becomes harder to work with for a
reason nobody can name — because the *conditions* changed rather than the code. Traffic grew tenfold,
the free tier's limits moved, the model provider deprecated the endpoint. This is why the expiry
condition exists and why it should contain a number: it is the only mechanism that turns a slow
environmental change into a discrete trigger. Day 232 (day-2 operations) is a whole day on this shape
of problem.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves.** *"Your 'what would make us change our minds' says
'if performance becomes a problem'. Whose performance, measured how, above what number? Right now
this ADR can never be wrong, which means it can never be reviewed."*

**The signal you would alert on.** Normally none — but the good ADRs in this plan are written so that
their expiry condition *is* something already measured. When the condition is "if p99 latency for
`/predict` exceeds 400 ms sustained", the alert already exists, and the ADR becomes a document the
alert points at. That is the ideal, and it is rare enough to be worth aiming at deliberately: **an
expiry condition that a monitor can evaluate is a decision that reviews itself.**

**Blast radius.** Writing an ADR changes one new file in `docs/adr/` and appends one row to
`docs/DECISIONS.md`. No capability is added. The one real hazard is *editing an existing ADR* instead
of superseding it, which silently rewrites the reasoning history and cannot be detected by reading
the file — only by reading `git log` on it. Bounded by git, which is why the ADR is a committed file
rather than a wiki page.

**Cost:** `0` requests, `0` tokens, `0` MB resident. A few kilobytes of Markdown per decision.

**The interview question.** *"Tell me about a technical decision you made that turned out to be
wrong."* The answer they want is not the mistake — it is whether you noticed, how, and how long it
took. If you can say "we'd written down that we'd revisit it if X went above Y, X went above Y in
March, and we did", you have described a mature engineering practice in one sentence.
