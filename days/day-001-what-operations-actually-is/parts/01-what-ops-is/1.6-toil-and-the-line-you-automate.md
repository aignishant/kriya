# 1.6 — Toil, and the line you automate

## One-line answer

Toil is manual work that scales linearly with the size of the system and produces no lasting value —
and the operator's core judgement is not *"can this be automated?"* but *"what happens when the
automation is wrong?"*

---

## The story

🎬 **The scene.** Every Monday, someone on a team opens a spreadsheet, copies eleven numbers out of
six different screens, pastes them into an email, and sends it. It takes most of the morning. They
have done it for two years.

😬 **The naive fix.** Automate it. Write a script that pulls the eleven numbers and sends the email.
It takes two days to write and it works.

💥 **Why it fails.** Not immediately — that is the pattern by now. The script runs every Monday for
nine months. In month ten, one of the six systems changes a field name, and the script sends the
email with a blank where number seven should be. Nobody notices, because nobody reads the email
carefully; they read it to check it arrived. In month twelve a decision is made on the basis of a
number that has been blank since month ten.

💡 **The insight.** The manual version had an accidental property nobody had named: **a human looked
at every number every week.** Slowly, expensively, and with a person who would have said "that's
odd" if number seven vanished. The automation removed the work *and* the inspection, and only the
work was written down as the requirement. Automating a task means you must now automate the noticing
too — otherwise you have not removed the labour, you have converted it into a silent failure.

---

## The idea in plain language

**Toil** is a specific kind of work, and the definition is worth being precise about because the word
gets used loosely to mean "work I don't enjoy". Work is toil when it is:

- **manual** — a human does it;
- **repetitive** — it has been done before and will be again;
- **automatable** — a machine could, in principle, do it;
- **tactical** — it is reactive, not planned;
- **devoid of lasting value** — the system is in the same state afterwards as before, just later;
- **linear with system size** — twice the system means twice the work.

That last one is the important one. Work that does not grow with the system is a chore. Work that
grows with the system is a **ceiling**, and it is the reason a team of five can operate ten services
and cannot operate a hundred.

Restarting a stuck process is toil. Manually approving a routine deploy is toil. Copying eleven
numbers into an email is toil. Writing a runbook is *not* toil — it is a lasting artifact that makes
future work cheaper. Investigating a novel failure is not toil either, even though it is reactive,
because you learn something that changes the system.

### The judgement, which is the actual skill

Everyone can see that toil should be automated. The interesting question is the one the story asks:
**what happens when the automation is wrong?**

Three outcomes, and they need completely different treatment:

| If the automation misfires, the result is… | Then | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **visible and reversible** | automate freely | regenerating a report, rebuilding an index, restarting a stateless process |
| **visible and irreversible** | automate with a gate — a human approves the action, not the analysis | deleting data, rotating a key, scaling to zero |
| **invisible** | **do not automate until you have built the detection first** | anything that can silently produce a wrong-but-plausible output |

The third row is the one people get wrong, and it is the story's row. It is also, by no coincidence,
where every AI-shaped failure in this curriculum lives: a model that returns a confident number for
garbage input, an agent that reports success on a task it did not do, a drift detector that has
stopped detecting.

**This is Principle 13 — blast radius before capability — in its most everyday form.** The rule is
not "be careful". The rule is: *the automation and the check that the automation worked land in the
same change.* Never in a later one, because there is no later one.

### Automation is not the only answer to toil

Three other answers, all legitimate, all under-used:

**Eliminate the work.** The best script is the one you did not need. If a process gets stuck weekly
and you automate the restart, you have made the symptom cheap and the cause invisible — and it will
now never be fixed. Sometimes the right response to toil is to ask why the task exists.

**Make it self-service.** If four teams ask you to do the same thing, the answer may be a documented
command they run themselves, not a script you own and maintain forever.

**Do it less often.** Some weekly tasks are weekly for no reason anybody remembers.

## Why Kriya needs it

Day 175 is *"Automated remediation — one action, with brakes you have tested."* Day 188 and Day 194
are the same subject for agents.

Those days build a system that observes a problem and fixes it without a human. That is the most
dangerous capability in this entire curriculum, and it is trap #4 from the plan's §5.1 — *autonomy
with no brake*. What makes it survivable is entirely decided by what you learn here: the remediation
ships with a rate limit (it may act at most once per window), an audit trail (every action is
recorded with what it saw), a kill switch (one command stops it), and a check that the remediation
*worked* rather than merely ran.

You also need it on Day 13, far sooner. CI automates "did somebody run the checks?" — and the trap is
identical: if the pipeline reports green when it actually skipped the test step, you have automated
the labour and removed the noticing.

## The mechanism

The mechanism is a rule you can apply on paper before you write anything, plus a demonstration of
what "automate the noticing too" looks like at the smallest possible scale.

Here is toil in this repository: checking that every day folder has a hub and a checklist. You could
do it by eye. It takes seconds today and will take an afternoon at Day 200 — **linear with system
size**, which makes it toil by the definition above.

```bash
# The manual version: look, and hope you notice.
ls days/*/LESSON.md days/*/CHECKLIST.md
```

**Line by line:** `ls` with two globs prints every hub and every checklist that exists. **It is
completely silent about the ones that do not**, which is precisely the failure mode of the manual
version: absence is invisible, and absence is what you were checking for. This is the single most
common flaw in hand-rolled operational checks, and it is worth having felt once.

Now the automated version — which already exists, because Day 0 built it:

```bash
uv run python scripts/depth_check.py 0
```

**Line by line:**

- `scripts/depth_check.py` is the repository's depth-contract checker. Given a day number it inspects
  that day's folder against the plan's §17: parts present, numbering contiguous, required sections in
  order, no clocks, no unmarked billable command, a hub that does not teach.
- **The important design property is that it reports absence.** It does not list what exists; it
  states what is missing and exits non-zero. That is the difference between the two blocks, and it is
  the whole lesson: an automated check must be built around what should be true, not around what is
  there.
- It takes a day number rather than a path, so a folder can be renamed without breaking it — the
  Day 0, part [4.3](../../../day-000-toolchain-skeleton-driver/parts/04-the-driver/4.3-the-dispatcher-and-resolving-a-day.md)
  rule that the number is the identity and the slug is a label.

Observed on **2026-08-24**:

```text
OK   day   0  19 parts

depth contract: all 1 checked days pass
```

And the same check against a day that is missing its hub — the state this repository was actually in
while Day 0 was being written:

```text
FAIL day   0  2 problems
       - LESSON.md: missing - every day needs a hub
       - CHECKLIST.md: missing

depth contract: 0/1 days pass
```

**That is what "automate the noticing" produces.** Two lines naming exactly what is absent, and a
non-zero exit code so a pipeline can refuse. The `ls` version would have printed a shorter list and
looked fine.

## When it breaks

The failure has a name — **automation complacency** — and it looks like this. From this repository's
own incident ledger, row 4, on 2026-08-24:

```text
$ ./o check
All checks passed!
40 files already formatted
no tests ran in 0.02s
OK   day   0  19 parts

depth contract: all 1 checked days pass
traceability: 0/279 closed, 0 problem(s); index: 279 IDs
OK all green
```

**What it says:** the test step passed.
**What it actually means:** the test step **ran no tests**. `pytest` exits with status `5` for *"no
tests were collected"*, and `o` forgives exit 5 with `|| [ $? -eq 5 ]` because before the first test
exists an empty suite genuinely is not a failure. So for the whole of Day 0, the green on that line
was produced by an exemption rather than by a passing test — and nobody would have noticed if the
test runner had been broken the entire time.

The way this was caught is the only way it is ever caught: **by breaking it on purpose.** Adding a
test containing `assert 1 == 2` produced:

```text
FAILED tests/test_probe.py::test_deliberate - assert 1 == 2
```

…which proved the exemption does not swallow a genuine failure. That is Principle 11 — every gate
must be able to go red — and it is why it is a principle rather than a suggestion.

**The smallest fix** here is not code: it is knowing that the exemption expires the moment real tests
exist, which is [Day 3](../../../day-003-pulse-v0/LESSON.md). From that day on, "no tests collected"
would be a silent regression rather than a fair description, and part
[4.1 of Day 3](../../../day-003-pulse-v0/LESSON.md) closes it.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** Every piece of automation they ship
carries four things, and they will not merge it without them: a **bound** on how often it can act, an
**audit record** of what it saw and what it did, a **kill switch** that a tired person can operate in
one command, and a **post-condition check** that the action achieved its purpose. An auto-remediator
without the fourth is the most dangerous thing in the estate, because it *reports success on having
acted* rather than on having fixed.

**What degrades at scale.** The bound. A remediation that restarts an unhealthy instance is sensible
for one instance and catastrophic across a fleet when the cause is a shared dependency: the automation
restarts everything, simultaneously, and turns a partial outage into a total one. This is why Day 175
specifies a rate limit before it specifies an action, and why Kubernetes' own equivalent — a pod
disruption budget, Day 45 — exists.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Automation that is correct and *too fast*. A human
doing a task at human speed provides accidental rate limiting and an accidental review at every step.
Remove the human and a wrong decision is executed across the whole system before anyone can read the
first alert. Speed is a capability, and by Principle 13 it needs a bound in the same change that
introduces it.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves.** *"This runs on a cron and logs on failure. Who reads
that log? If the answer is nobody, this job will be silently broken for months and we'll find out
from a customer. Make it alert on not having succeeded recently, not on having failed."* That
inversion — alert on absence of success, not on presence of failure — is one of the most valuable
sentences in operations, and Day 77 makes it a rule.

**The signal you would alert on.** For any automation: **time since last successful run**, and
**actions taken per window**. The first catches the job that silently stopped; the second catches the
job that has gone berserk. Notice that neither is "did it fail" — a job that fails loudly is the
healthy case, because you find out.

**Blast radius.** This part introduces no new capability; the two commands are read-only inspections
of files in `days/`. But it introduces the *habit* that governs every capability in the plan. Stated
in the plan's own form: worst case for an unbounded automation is that it performs a wrong action at
machine speed across the whole system; who can trigger it is anyone or anything that can produce the
input it watches, including an attacker; what bounds it is a rate limit, an approval gate and a kill
switch, all of which must exist before it is switched on.

**Cost:** `0` requests, `0` tokens, `0` MB resident. The depth check is a local Python process reading
Markdown.

**The interview question.** *"When would you choose not to automate something?"* The answer that
lands: when the failure would be invisible and I haven't built the detection yet; when the task is
rare enough that the automation would rot between uses and be trusted anyway; and when the manual
process is doing a second job nobody wrote down — usually a human sanity-check — that the automation
would silently delete.
