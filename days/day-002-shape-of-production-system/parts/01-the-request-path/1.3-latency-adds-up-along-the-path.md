# 1.3 — Latency adds up along the path

## One-line answer

A response cannot be faster than the sum of its steps, so the only useful latency budget is one that
is **shared out across the path in advance**, and the step you cannot control has to be given its
number first rather than last.

---

## The story

🎬 **The scene.** You have an appointment at nine in the morning on the other side of town. You work
backwards. The walk to the bus stop takes ten minutes, the bus takes twenty-five, and the walk at the
other end takes five. You set an alarm.

😬 **The naive fix.** Add the three numbers up, add fifteen minutes to be safe, and set the alarm for
that.

💥 **Why it fails.** You are late. Not because any single leg was much worse than expected. The walk
was three minutes over because you could not find your keys, the bus turned up four minutes late, and
then it sat in traffic for eighteen. No individual leg was a disaster. **The fifteen minutes of safety
was handed to the journey as a whole, and the first two overruns ate it before the leg that actually
varies.**

💡 **The insight.** A budget for a journey is not one number. It is a number *per leg*, and the legs
you do not control need the largest share. Anybody who makes that journey every day knows this without
being told. They do not think "forty minutes to get there". They think "the bus can lose twenty
minutes in traffic" and work outward from that. The variation lives in specific places, and pretending
it is spread evenly is how you end up late.

---

## The idea in plain language

**Latency** is how long something takes, measured from the point of view of whoever is waiting. For a
request that passes through several steps, where each step waits for the next, the total is at least
the sum of the parts. There is no clever way around addition.

There are three words you need, and people mix them up constantly.

**Latency** is how long one request takes, measured in milliseconds.

**Throughput** is how many requests you handle per second. These two are *different*, and improving
one often makes the other worse. Batching ten requests together raises throughput and raises the
latency of the first one in the batch. Day 110 makes that trade real for model serving.

**Saturation** is how full the system is. A system at 50% utilisation has queues that are almost
always empty. The same system at 95% has queues that are frequently long, and **latency rises
non-linearly with utilisation rather than in proportion to it**. That is why "we have plenty of
headroom, we are only at 90%" is one of the more expensive sentences in operations, and it is Day 85's
subject.

### Sharing out the budget

Suppose `pulse` promises to answer `/assist` in under three seconds for most requests. That is one
number and it is not yet useful. Turn it into a budget.

| Step | Budget | Why that number |
| --- | --- | --- |
| network in and out | 100 ms | outside your control, so measure it rather than assuming |
| `API`, doing routing, validation and serialisation | 20 ms | your own code doing small work |
| `DB`, fetching the ticket | 50 ms | one indexed lookup |
| `INDEX`, retrieving similar tickets | 200 ms | a vector search over a modest index |
| `LLM`, generating the reply | **2 400 ms** | **the step you do not control** |
| slack | 230 ms | the margin, allocated *last* rather than first |
| **total** | **3 000 ms** | the promise |

**Read the shape of the table rather than its numbers.** Eighty percent of the budget goes to the one
step you cannot profile, cannot optimise and cannot restart. That is not pessimism. It is the correct
allocation, and it has two immediate consequences that a single total would never have surfaced.

**Optimising your own code is nearly pointless here.** Halving the `API` step from 20 ms to 10 ms
improves the total by a third of one percent. Engineers spend weeks on that step, because it is the
one they can see.

**The only real lever is the `LLM` step, and the levers on it are not "make it faster".** They are:
cache the answer so that you do not make the call at all, which is Day 133; start streaming so that
the *perceived* latency is time to first token rather than time to completion, which is Day 131; or
answer without it and degrade gracefully, which is Day 129. Every one of those is an architectural
choice, and every one of them is visible from the budget table and invisible from the total.

### Sequential and parallel

The `DB` and `INDEX` steps in that table do not depend on each other. Run them at the same time and
the path costs `max(50, 200) = 200 ms` instead of `250 ms`. On this budget that saves 50 ms, which is
worth almost nothing, and saying so is the point. **Running steps in parallel is the optimisation
people reach for first and it is almost never where the time is.** Look at the budget, find the
biggest number, and work on that.

There is one place where parallelism changes the *shape* rather than the size, and it is worth knowing
now. If you fan out to several steps and wait for all of them, your latency is the **slowest** one, so
your p99 is dominated by whichever backend has the worst tail. That is tail latency amplification, and
it is Day 63.

## Why Kriya needs it

Day 73 is *"Service level objectives: the number, the window, and what it costs to promise it."*

An SLO for `pulse` will be a sentence like *"99% of `/predict` requests are answered successfully in
under 400 ms over a rolling four-week window."* Before you can write that sentence honestly you have
to know that it is *achievable*, and the only way to know is to have a budget per step and evidence
for every number in it. A promise made without a budget is a promise you will discover you cannot keep
during the first busy week.

Day 110, on serving latency for the model, and Day 131, on streaming and time to first token, are both
direct consequences of the allocation exercise above.

## The mechanism

You cannot measure `pulse`'s steps, because it does not exist. You **can** measure the shape of the
problem, and doing so with real numbers is what stops the budget table from being a fantasy.

```python
# lab/budget.py — allocate a latency budget across a path and see what a change is worth.
BUDGET_MS = 3_000

path = [
    ("network in + out", 100),
    ("API routing + validation", 20),
    ("DB fetch ticket", 50),
    ("INDEX retrieve similar", 200),
    ("LLM generate", 2_400),
]

spent = sum(ms for _, ms in path)
for name, ms in path:
    share = ms / spent
    saving = 0.5 * ms / spent
    print(f"{name:28s} {ms:>6,d} ms  {share:6.1%} of the path   halving it buys {saving:5.1%}")
print(f"{'spent':28s} {spent:>6,d} ms")
print(f"{'slack':28s} {BUDGET_MS - spent:>6,d} ms")
```

**Line by line:**

- `BUDGET_MS = 3_000` is the promise, written as one named constant. The underscore is a digit
  separator, which Python ignores and human eyes do not. On a number with more digits it is the
  difference between reading `30000` correctly and reading it wrong.
- `path` is a list of tuples rather than a dictionary, because **order matters here**. This is a path
  rather than a set, and a list preserves the order explicitly rather than relying on insertion order
  as an incidental property.
- `spent = sum(ms for _, ms in path)` is a generator expression, with `_` standing in for the value
  being thrown away. Summing before the loop means each row can be expressed as a share of the real
  total rather than of the budget, which is the honest denominator.
- `saving = 0.5 * ms / spent` works out **what halving that step is worth to the total**, which is the
  entire point of the script. A step that is 1% of the path can be made infinitely fast and buy you
  0.5%.
- `{ms:>6,d}` right-aligns the number in six columns with a thousands separator, and `{share:6.1%}`
  formats it as a percentage to one decimal place. Aligning the columns matters more than it looks,
  because the argument this script is making is about *relative size*, and misaligned numbers hide it.

Run it:

```

API routing + validation         20 ms    0.7% of the path   halving it buys  0.4%

```

**That last column is the whole lesson in one number.** Halving every single step you own, meaning
network, API, database and index together, buys you 6.7%. Halving the one you do not own buys 43.3%.

**And notice that the shares do not add up to 100%.** They add up to 99.9%, because each one is
rounded to one decimal place. That deserves a second's attention rather than a shrug. A report whose
rows do not sum to its total is the normal state of any rounded breakdown, and knowing that is what
stops you hunting for a missing step that does not exist.

## When it breaks

The failure is a budget that was never allocated, discovered as a symptom nobody can pin down. Here is
what it looks like in a real request, and it is worth reading carefully, because the numbers go
against instinct.

```text
$ curl -sS -o /dev/null -w 'dns=%{time_namelookup}s connect=%{time_connect}s total=%{time_total}s\n' https://pypi.org/pypi/ruff/json
dns=0.005038s connect=0.063184s total=52.926316s
```

Observed on **2026-08-24** on this machine.

**What it says:** the request took fifty-two seconds.

**What it actually means:** name resolution took five *milliseconds* and connecting took sixty-three
milliseconds. Together they are one tenth of one percent of the total. Everything else, meaning
fifty-two and a half seconds, happened after the connection was open, on a link this machine does not
control.

**Why this matters more than it looks:** if the only number you had was `total`, the natural next step
is to start investigating DNS, because DNS is famously the cause of everything and it is easy to
check. You would have spent an hour ruling out a step that accounted for 0.01% of the problem. **The
breakdown is not a nicety. It is the difference between an hour and a glance.**

**The smallest fix** is not to make anything faster. It is to record the breakdown at all, which for
`curl` is one `-w` flag, and for a service is one span per outbound call, which is Day 69.

The second failure mode is subtler and it is the one that ends up in a postmortem: **a budget that
adds up correctly and is measured with the wrong statistic**. If each step's *average* is within
budget, the total average is within budget, and your p99 can still be catastrophically over, because
the slow requests at each step are not the same requests, and averages are completely blind to that.
Budget in percentiles or do not budget at all. That is Day 63.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** They set an explicit **timeout per
step**, derived from the budget, and they make the timeouts *decrease* as you go inward. An outer call
that allows three seconds must not wait on an inner call that allows five, or the outer timeout is
decorative. That discipline has a name, **deadline propagation**, and it means the remaining budget
travels with the request, so each step is told how long it has left rather than being given a fixed
number from a config file. It is one of the highest-leverage things in a distributed system, and it is
routinely absent.

**What degrades at scale.** Queuing. Every number in that budget table is a *service* time, meaning
how long the work takes once it starts. Under load, requests wait before they start, and the waiting
is invisible to the component doing the work. Your database reports a 5 ms query while the caller
experiences 300 ms, because 295 of those milliseconds were spent in a connection pool. **Measure at
the caller, always**, and treat a component's self-reported timing as a lower bound. Day 47 on
connection pools and Day 85 on capacity are where this becomes concrete.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Retries turning a slow step into an outage. A step
is slow, the caller times out and retries, the retry adds load to the already-struggling step, more
calls time out, and the load multiplies. This is a **retry storm**, and the arithmetic is brutal:
three retries per call means a struggling dependency receives four times its normal traffic at exactly
the moment it can least handle it. The containment is bounded retries with exponential backoff and
jitter, plus a circuit breaker that stops calling entirely for a while. Day 130 builds it against a
rate-limited provider, and Principle 13 says the bound ships with the retry rather than after it.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves.** *"You have added a call to the index inside the
request path with a two-second timeout, on a route whose whole budget is three seconds. What is the
caller's timeout? If it is also three seconds, we have guaranteed that a slow index turns into a hard
failure rather than a degraded answer, and we said degradation was acceptable here."*

**The signal you would alert on.** **A high percentile of end-to-end latency, split by route**, and
separately **time spent per downstream dependency**. That is two signals, and the division of labour
matters. The first tells you a user is having a bad time and is worth waking somebody for. The second
tells you where, and is never worth waking anybody for on its own. Alert on the symptom and put the
cause on a dashboard, which is Day 77. And do not alert on averages, because an average latency alert
will not fire during an outage that affects five percent of users, and five percent of users is an
outage.

**Blast radius:** none from this part. The script is arithmetic in a scratch file, and it reads nothing
and calls nothing. The `curl` in the failure section makes one read-only request to a public index over
the internet. The worst case is that it is slow, which is the point.

**Cost:** `0` model calls and `0` tokens. One HTTPS request of a few hundred kilobytes to `pypi.org`,
and a Python process that exits immediately, leaving `0` MB resident.

**The interview question.** *"Your p99 latency is 3 seconds and your SLO is 1 second. Walk me through
how you would find out why."* The answer they are listening for is decomposition before investigation:
break the path into steps, find which step owns the tail, and only then start optimising. Mention that
you would check queuing at the caller before believing any component's self-reported timing.
